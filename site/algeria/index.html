Where the dots come from

OpenCelliD is a free map built by volunteers. Their phones note which cell they are connected to and where the phone is (from GPS), and upload it. From many such reports, OpenCelliD estimates where each cell is. A real tower usually carries several cells (different operators, generations and directions), so we grouped cells of the same operator that are less than 150 m apart into one site.

How accurate it is

Positions are estimates from phones, not from the operators. They can be off by tens to a few hundred metres, and two nearby towers can be merged into one dot. Busy areas with many volunteers are well covered; quiet areas can be empty even if towers exist there. The long lines of dots along highways (for example Algiers–Sétif–Constantine) are where volunteers drove with the app switched on, not a sign that towers only stand along roads.

What the map does not show

Which towers are connected to each other by microwave beams, and how strong those beams are over time. That is exactly what measuring rain needs, and only the operators have it. This map shows where beams could exist, not where they do.
