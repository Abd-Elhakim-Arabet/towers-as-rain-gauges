Phone towers can measure rain.

Phone towers talk to each other with invisible radio beams. When it rains, the raindrops make the beam a little weaker. Phone companies already record how strong every beam is, every few seconds. We use those records to work out how much rain is falling, street by street, and to warn people before a flash flood.

Why Algeria is a simulation

For Algeria we know where the phone towers are. That comes from a free public map called OpenCelliD. What we do not have is the record of how strong each beam was over time. Phone companies keep that private. Without it we cannot measure real Algerian rain with beams yet.

What we have for Algeria

  • Where the towers are (Mobilis, Djezzy, Ooredoo). These positions come from the public, so they can be off by a few hundred metres.
  • Real rainfall from past storms, measured by NASA satellites.
  • Maps of the land: hills, valleys, buildings, roads, where people live.

What we do not have

  • Which towers are connected by a radio beam. Some use cables instead.
  • Details of each beam, like its length and frequency.
  • The beam-strength records. These are what actually show the rain.

What we do instead

  • Teach and test the AI on real beam records from Sweden, Italy and the Netherlands.
  • Draw realistic beams between the real Algerian tower positions.
  • Take a real Algerian storm and calculate how it would have weakened those beams. Then run our system on that.

So the Algeria demo shows how the system would work there. It does not prove how accurate it would be there. To prove that, a phone company would need to share records from 50 to 100 beams in one city for a trial, and we would compare them with the national weather service's rain gauges.

How a beam becomes a flood warning

Right now these steps use fixed rules. This is the "simple method" whose scores are shown further down. Next we replace the weakest steps with AI.

step 1

Read the beam strength

The phone company already saves it every few seconds for every beam.

step 2

Is it raining?

Rain makes the signal shaky. If it shakes a lot, we say it is raining.

step 3

Find the normal level

We remember how strong the beam was just before the rain. Anything weaker than that is caused by rain.

step 4

Ignore wet antennas

Water sitting on the antenna also weakens the beam. We subtract that, so we only count rain in the air.

step 5

Turn it into rain

A standard formula from the ITU converts "how much weaker" into "how many mm of rain per hour".

How good is the simple method?

We set it up using June and July 2015, then tested it on August 2015, a month it had never seen. It is like practising on old exams and then sitting a new one. These August scores are the ones our AI has to beat.

"Match" is a correlation: 0 means no relation, 1 means a perfect match.

It notices most rain
Out of 100 rainy 5-minute periods, it noticed 71. The ones it missed were mostly light drizzle, which barely changes the beam.
It rarely gives false alarms
Out of 100 dry periods, it wrongly said "rain" fewer than 5 times.
The rain amounts are close
Hour by hour, beam rain and gauge rain go up and down together (match 0.81). The typical mistake is about 1 mm per hour. Over the whole month the beams measured 6% less rain than the gauges.
As good as a weather radar
The weather radar matched the same gauges at 0.80. The beams did as well, with no radar, using equipment that already exists.
What still needs work
Three things. Minute-by-minute estimates are noisy. To decide whether it is raining, the method looks 30 minutes into the future, which is too late for live warnings. And two of its settings had to be tuned by hand. The AI is meant to fix all three.

Choosing when to say "it is raining"

We tried many settings for "how shaky counts as rain" and for how many minutes to look at. Higher on the chart is better. The best setting was 0.35 while looking at 60 minutes.

looking at 15 min30 min60 min

The wet-antenna correction matters a lot

Each bar shows how far off the total rain was. 0% is perfect, bars to the right mean too much rain, bars to the left too little. Without any correction, the beams report 3.5 times too much rain. The setting we chose was 12% low in June–July and 6% low in August.

Removing broken beams helps a lot

A few broken beams spoil the overall numbers. We removed 45 of the 728, choosing them using June–July only. That almost doubles the 5-minute match with the radar.
