Every cell-tower link is already a rain gauge.

Rain weakens the microwave signal between two towers, and operators already log that signal every few seconds. We turn the loss in decibels into rainfall in mm/h, then into flash-flood alerts for neighbourhoods that have no rain gauge and no radar.

Why the Algeria part is a simulation

Algeria does have open data on cell towers, but it is the wrong kind of data for measuring rain. OpenCelliD gives us where towers are. Measuring rain needs the signal logs of the microwave links between towers, and those stay inside the operators' network systems.

Open for Algeria

  • Tower positions from OpenCelliD (MCC 603: Mobilis, Djezzy, Ooredoo). These are crowdsourced estimates, accurate to tens or hundreds of metres.
  • Satellite rainfall from past storms (GPM IMERG, 10 km, 30 min).
  • Terrain, land cover, population, roads.

Not open anywhere

  • Which towers are linked by microwave and which use fibre.
  • Each link's frequency, length and polarisation.
  • The received and transmitted signal levels (RSL/TSL) over time. This is the actual rain signal.

So we

  • Train and score the AI on real link data from Sweden, Italy and the Netherlands.
  • Draw plausible links between real Algerian tower sites, with lengths and frequencies matching real networks.
  • Feed real storm rainfall through the physics of attenuation, add realistic noise, and run the same pipeline.

The Algeria demo shows how the system would behave on a real network there. It is not evidence of accuracy in Algeria. That needs a pilot with an operator: 50 to 100 links in one flood-prone wilaya, compared against ONM rain gauges.

dB → mm/hpipeline

From a signal log to a flood alert

Each step below is the classical method that Phase 1 reproduced. Phase 2 replaces the first and the last of these steps with small neural networks.

RSL, TSL · dBm

Signal log

Operators record received and transmitted power for every link, every 10 s to 15 min.

std over 60 min

Is it raining?

Rain makes the signal jitter. A link is flagged wet when its rolling spread passes a threshold.

dB

Dry baseline

The signal level just before rain started is held as the reference. Loss above it is rain.

dB

Wet antenna

Water on the antenna covers adds loss that is not rain along the path. It is subtracted.

A = a·Rᵇ·L

Rain rate

ITU-R P.838 turns loss per km into mm/h, using the link's frequency and polarisation.

Phase 3 · mm/h on a 1 km grid

Rain map + 1 h nowcast

Many links together give a rain map. Optical flow pushes it forward 30 to 60 min.

Phase 5 · risk level

Flood risk

Rain is combined with terrain, wadi channels and paved surfaces into a risk score per neighbourhood and road.

Phase 6 · AR / FR / EN

Ranked alerts

Alerts are ranked by people and critical sites exposed. No flood depth is predicted.

r, MCCbaseline

The classical baseline: what it scored

Tuned on June and July 2015, scored once on August 2015. August was never used for tuning, so these are honest out-of-sample numbers. They are the bar the AI models must clear.

Rain or no rain: MCC 0.58
MCC runs from 0 (no better than chance) to 1 (perfect). At 0.58, the link catches 71% of the 5-minute periods the radar calls rainy, and wrongly flags rain in under 5% of the dry ones. Most misses are very light rain at the edges of storms, where the signal barely moves.
How much rain: r 0.81 against gauges
Hour by hour, link rainfall and gauge rainfall move together closely (r = 1 would be identical). The typical hourly error is about 1 mm, and over the month the links come out 6% below the gauges.
As good as the radar, at this scale
The weather radar scored r 0.80 against the same gauges. The links matched it without any radar, using equipment the operator already runs.
Where it falls short
At 5-minute resolution the scatter is wide (r 0.58), the rain detector needs 30 minutes of future data, and it relies on two hand-tuned settings. Those three weaknesses are what Phase 2's AI models target.

Choosing the rain detector

Agreement with radar (MCC) on June–July for each window length and threshold. The best is a 60-min window at 0.35 dB.

15 min30 min60 min

The wet-antenna setting swings the total

Bias against gauges on June–July for each correction. With no correction, links report 3.5× too much rain. The chosen one is −12% on tuning and −6% on the test month.

Effect of dropping faulty links

Pooled scores across all links are dragged down by a few faulty ones. Dropping 45 of 728 sub-links, chosen using June–July only, nearly doubles the 5-minute correlation with radar. The median link was fine either way.
